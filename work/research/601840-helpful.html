Early Access Review

I just finished my first play session.

I was not expecting this when I bought the game.

I am a big fan of Slay the Spire, and I recently got Monster Train, and I felt like that was the next step in the genre, the next move forward. The same thing, but innovated enough to be different.

And then I found Griftlands.

Griftlands is the new king. It feels like a fully fledged RPG roguelike, the factions playing off each other, the bonuses for being loved/hated. Everything you got in Slay is still present. The random encounters. Artifacts. Elites. Its just presented in a far more engaging style, almost like a single player tabletop campaign that is modular and is different on every playthrough.

It feels like im playing a little bit of all the great games I love. Moving around the map reminds me of what Hex should have been. I get a Deep Sky Derelict feel from the combat, but thats not very accurate so I dont knwo why.

I really enjoy the negotiation game play, and combat is more of what you would expect from this genre. Not bad, not good. But still fun and engaging with lots of different archetypes and play styles to explore.

I cannot express this enough. If you are a fan of the genre, or even tcgs in general... If the idea of shuffling cards excites you, I strongly recommend this game.

EDIT: Ive reset all my progress to start fresh to play the full release. Its like visiting old friends but none of them remember me. (thats an inside joke)

Early Access Review

This game is fantastic!

This game takes the deck building, card battling mechanics of Slay the Spire and wraps it up into a fun interactive story format where choices matter. The deck building and card battles are still the core of the game. However now instead of simply following a line on a map going from one encounter to the next, there's a a visual treat of a world for the story to play out on. You'll make decisions that will matter in the game.

Some of the ways decisions have impacted me are: Making someone hate me and now whenever they're nearby I suffer some penalty from them buttin' in. Someone else likes me and will help and provide a bonus. I helped someone out of a jam and every so often they send me a gift. It's also easier to negotiate with someone who loves me than someone who hates me. The love/hate status is also important in that each character will provide a bonus if they love you or a penalty if they hate you. These aren't permanent in that it seems possible that relationship statuses can change.

You're also not alone in that you may have characters temporarily join your team for one encounter or a series of encounters. You can temporarily hire people and costs will depend on relationship status. You can get a pet and train them. There are also one-time rewards that allow you to summon a creature for a battle.

The card battles are also fun. There's two types - battles and negotiations. I really like the negotiation card battles but I do have trouble with the keywords/concepts and what they do. I still find myself having to pause and check the arguments and what they do. It's something that I'm sure will become more familiar enough over time. One feature that I wanted to mention because I love it is that cards gain xp when played. When that bar is filled the card will evolve and you'll be presented with a choice. So even basic cards can become better over time.

There's so much more that I could go on about this game. I'm just really impressed with what Klei has done with this. They have managed to take what could have easily been a mediocre "yet another deck builder" and created something fabulous.

P.S. I can't believe I forgot to mention this. First, there's a panic threshold in which an enemy will either run away or stop fighting. When the fight is over you're given the choice for each opponent in either killing them or accepting surrender. For every character there is an item you will receive if you kill them. However depending on the situation there may be ramifications for choosing to kill the opponent. There's a guide that goes into this in much further detail. However it's yet another example of how awesome this game is.

Early Access Review

Updated for Flourish & Mettle Update

My opinion of Griftlands is largely unchanged from my original review (below). The combat is tight and you're frequently just a misplay or two away from losing a big fight, but the RPG elements get increasingly gimmicky the more you play. It's sort of sad that choosing whether to help someone or not really depends on whether the passive bonus they give for loving or hating you is important or not. At higher prestige runs then you feel somewhat punished for taking an in-character action that ends up giving you a malus - I understand that's a strategic trade off you have to make, but it highlights where the RPG and strategic aspects clash jarringly in Griftlands.

My real disappointment with Griftlands though was the introduction of Mettle as an in-game currency. Hades uses a similar concept where continuous play allows you to get stronger and thus more likely to succeed at runs, but Hades uses this because Hades' game goal is to allow you to experience more story. Hades locks story behind walls for you to conquer, and giving you Darkness to boost your base character's stats made a lot of sense because you're still striving towards more story even after finishing a run. But finishing Griftlands story isn't hard in the slightest, even if you're playing at the lowest prestige level (there's also an even easier story mode). Sure it'll take you a few runs to get your first win, but once you've got there there's nothing more to see, the story's complete. It becomes challenge for challenge's sake and the Mettle just makes the challenge easier over time. Instead of teaching players to get better then you can just hit your head against the wall until you have enough Mettle boosting your base stats to give you the edge. And Hades manages to hold your attention in runs with dazzling and weird combinations that let your gameplay style shift and adapt to new quirks and patterns of combat with the power ups you get during the run. By contrast, Griftlands combat is flat and "realistic", which I still see as a big shortcoming. In short, Griftlands just doesn't benefit from the Mettle system, It feels like it's pandering to people that want its weak RPG experience without the roguelite challenge, but you could just play story mode if you wanted that. Griftland offers a cop out to actually refining your strategic skill. I just don't know. It feels like a huge misstep.

On the positive, the Flourish system is well-implemented and fun without being overly power creepy.

Overall, I continue to recommend Griftlands because developer, Klei, has done something interesting with the genre. However, I maintain that Griftlands does not have meaningful longevity in gameplay once you've tried out the fairly obvious strategic paths for each character.

Original Review

Griftlands is fun... BUT... I do have a lot of concerns around its replayability and to explain this I need to take a quick dip into Slay The Spire.

When designing a game a developer has to decide how many resources they're going to commit to various aspects of the game. Slay The Spire focuses almost entirely on the card design and mechanical aspects of combat such that the combinations of powers and card effects lead to craziness and epicness akimbo. You'll have runs where you stack armour up to 500 and drop instant one shot spells constantly, or throw an endless stream of 50 damage shivs. These things are possible because all Slay The Spire cares about is the depth of its mechanics.

In Griftlands, developer Klei has split its resources to offer an RPG experience as well as a deckbuilder. To be clear, the RPG stuff is really good fun, and the way they've implemented it with the combat and negotiation and the love/hate system is sublime. HOWEVER, at least at this point in time, there's only so much to the RPG aspect of it. The quests you get shift a bit between runs, and the bosses are randomised, but you'll see a lot of what's on offer quest-wise after a few runs and I found myself skipping a lot of the quest text even after just two runs.

Now, that would be fine and cool if the mechanical combat depth that Slay The Spire does so well is on par, but sadly it's just not. By splitting the game development resources to add on this (initially superb) RPG element, they've taken away from the mechanical aspect that is the reason Slay The Spire is so engaging and replayable. What's worse is they've even implemented a brawl mode where you just do the battles and negotiations in quick fire (dispensing with the day-to-day travelling and chatter) so that players can focus on the mechanical aspect, which I do think is a nice option, but I also feel it exposes the lack of depth in available strategies and wacky combinations that comparatively exist in Slay The Spire and Monster Train. To counterbalance this, Klei has tried to create different characters with very different mechanical interactions in their combats and negotiations (i.e. I think there is a greater difference between Sal and Rook than there is between The Ironclad and The Silent), which is a bold undertaking, and I would say each character's design interesting and well thought out, but, again, another large drain on design resources that, I feel, ultimately stifles the depth of development into each individual.

Now it could be said that Griftlands is opting for a more "down-to-earth" approach to the combat madness, by which I mean I'm willing to believe it's a design choice that the interactions don't get absurdly out of control. You do still get fun combinations in Griftlands and they feel powerful and silly at times, but always with that edge of "if this was the real world then this would be believable" kind of approach. And maybe that more grounded approach has value, but I feel what makes the roguelite deckbuilding genre fun is the complete insanity in the strategies you can produce. Thus, if you're not going to do this kind of genre with that kind of selling point, then the RPG stuff has got to be really top notch and, again, it's good, but is it enough to make up for thinning down what I believe is the core aspect of this genre? I don't know. Maybe the compromise is for the best, but, again, do you play a deckbuilder to see the same RPG interactions? Or do you play it to make something busted happen in your deck? I think it's the latter.

To be clear, I do recommend Griftlands a lot and Klei are a solid, clever developer and I want to know where they'll take the game. I think the genre combination is really cool and fun, and while I've ragged on the mechanical design being a bit lacking, I do think that people who want a lighter experience than Slay The Spire's more hardcore approach will appreciate it. However, I remain concerned that it doesn't have the depth of gameplay that the deckbuilding roguelite genre has come to be known for in terms of available strategies and the wackiness of combinations, and, ultimately, I think that's what these gives this genre its draw.

Early Access Review

Griftlands is a big "maybe" for me, going from being super engaging to being a total dud. So let me open up some of my thoughts about the game, since it might be a good fit for you.

+ The art style, presentation, world and atmosphere is top notch. The characters are voiced and speak in their own alien gibberish. The voices convey emotion, the music fits the scene nicely and the graphics are outstanding. You really get sucked in into the sci-fi world. This is probably one of the nicest looking Klei games, and that's something.

+ Dual deck system is super fun. You build two decks that enable you to fight physically and socially. Both are fun in their own way, and completely different. Focusing too much on one will make the other weaker though, and you can't beat any boss characters with just speech. You can make them weaker though, but that's about it.

+ Allie and morality system. Help people and they will help you - be a murderous bastard and you will get penalized for it. This kind of an approach makes you think about what you could and should be doing, keeping the big picture in mind and forces you to stay your hand. Exectutions are very profitable but will make you take harsh penalties.

- The tooltips need work. The game is filled with huge amounts of status effects, conditions and keywords, but they aren't all explained in the heat of the moment. Having a pop-up tooltip when hovering over the keywords would be very useful to ease people in.

-/+ Story and length. For me this is actually a big minus, but for some it might be an icing on the cake. Griftlands has three characters with their own branching stories that take hours to complete. Each story is divided into several days that each consist of random encounters. For a roguelite game, the super long story is a big no-no, since restarting after taking a loss or making bad choices is such a pain in the ass that it severly reduces the urge to play the game. Even after winning I feel burnt out on the game and don't want to keep playing. It took me months between sessions to return to Griftlands after a single campaign win, and after taking a loss, it will probably stay away for months too.

See, I feel like it will take me too long to redo my progress and try to correct my errors. I couldn't care less about the story itself, since it's not that good or interesting, but about the encounters and choices. And if it will take me 2-3 hours to retrace some of my steps per session, it's way too much. Slay the Spire, Monster Train and the rest of their ilk have condenced a similar experience to 30-45 minutes, which leaves me wanting to replay the whole thing again.

In its current state Griftlands is beatiful, has a nice setting, some interesting mechanics but overstays its welcome every time I end up playing it. The experience isn't so good that I want to grind through the story to unlock things. All the character interactions are cute at first, but end up being utterly meaningless when encountered multiple times. It could be a good game if it wasn't so damn bloated with things that take focus away from actual encounters.

Worth a try, but not my favourite by far.

Once upon a time, there was a little game called Slay the Spire, which merged the perma-fail mechanics of a roguelike RPG with the mechanics of a card-battling deckbuilder. Slay the Spire wasn't the first game to breed these two disparate genres - that honor goes to Dream Quest - but it was the first game to get it "right," and over the following years dozens of imitators would crop up. Many of these imitators were rote, uninspired copycats. Others, like Shiny Shoe's Monster Train, flatly improved on the formula. Yet more, like Ground Shatter's Fights in Tight Spaces, did something interesting with it.

Klei's Griftlands is one of those interesting deckbuilding roguelikes.

The Hook
The hook for Griftlands is that it's not a linear dungeon, ascent, or mission, but more like a... "Mos Eisley simulator." Your character is in a sci-fi frontier town attempting to pursue a 3-day-long story mission (which is roughly linear, though with randomized bosses). As you wheel, deal, and murder your way to your goal amidst all the reprobates and ne'er-do-wells, you'll use two decks of drafted cards, representing your social abilities and battle abilities respectively.

The Game
Griftlands is, bizarrely, two interlinked but mechanically-separate card battler games, with distinctly different rules to each other, but the results of which can inform each other: for instance, if you kill enough people in the combat card game, then you'll get 'reputation' cards that increase the damage of your intimidation but lessen the effect of your negotiation in the social card game, and if you demoralize an opponent in the social card game by taunting them before a fight, it might weaken them in the combat card game.

Having a roguelike where half of the experience is talking to people necessitates a different philosophy to how one envisions "encounters," and that adds on another, second layer of what makes Griftlands strange and unique: its faction system. There are no "random bandit encounters." Rather, all quests and sidequests pull from a pool of the 282 named characters who live in the Griftlands, with distinct character designs and a couple of lines of backstory, as well as some friends and enemies that they're more and less likely to associate with. Griftlands isn't going to win any awards for its deep writing, but each of these characters has an associated faction and a job, and they're slotted into encounters where it would be appropriate for them to be. You never encounter "a merchant getting attacked by bandits:" you encounter the bandits Jace and Sekt going after the merchant Bennawem, and maybe you already did a quest where you cozied up with Jace and Sekt's boss, so you leave Bennawem to die to preserve the mechanical benefits you get from that relationship. It's not unlike doing favors for Animal Crossing villagers, but with a lot more murdering the other villagers.

This change to encounter philosophy is what makes Griftlands what it is. You're never quite empowered to wholly, nonviolently negotiate your way through the titular Griftlands because sometimes you're backed into a corner where you have to throw down, but nor can you just murder your way through indiscriminately, or you'll alienate everyone and make too many enemies and the encounters will get harder and harder. Although content is 'encounter-based' and procedurally-generated in the same way as Slay the Spire or Monster Train, Griftlands ends up feeling much more precarious, both because you're having to build two decks at the same time and not slouch on either of them, and because the game flow feeds into the narrative of the player as an amoral criminal barely a half-step in front of the consequences of their actions.

Griftlands is, compared to its deckbuilding roguelike peers, almost Too Much Game. Successful runs take several (~3-5) hours to complete, and with three different narrative campaigns with characters who have different decks from each other (so, six decks total, across 2 different deck-building systems), the game has so much upfront content that it's honestly daunting. It's much more of a commitment to get into than other deckbuilding roguelikes owing to all those moving parts, and the moving parts don't necessary make it better, just different. But what Griftlands lacks in accessibility, it makes up in style, and no deckbuilding roguelike made me feel like I was telling my own story quite like this one.

Early Access Review

My thoughts after 20h with the game, mostly in terms of comparison to Slay the Spire (StS from now on). I've only played the first character, with about half its card pool unlocked. Once I unlock all content I might edit this with some additional thoughts.

TLDR: this is a perfect example of how you can take inspiration from a game, but putting a new spin on it instead of just lazy cloning. In terms of STS-inspired games (more and more of them every day), this might be the one that's actually worth exploring in depth.

Basic idea
You pick a character (out of 2, the 3rd one is incoming) and progress through 5 days (Spire levels, if you will) of content, improving your 2 decks (combat+negotiation) before the final showdown against your nemesis. The start/end is the same per character, but there's branching in the mid-portion of the game. Win or lose, your score goes towards unlocking additional content for that character (mostly cards and some cosmetics) and you restart.
Once you understand the basic ideas, it's unlikely to lose a run, but there's also a long list of 'Ascension' levels to get through.
A run is significantly longer than a StS-one, especially if you don't breeze through the story part (more on this later). In that case expect 4-5 hours per run.

A story of two decks
The most obvious change to the StS-formula is that here you are managing two decks instead of one.
I won't go into too much detail about combat, because it's combat with the usual trappings. I will say that it will seem a bit more shallow at first than StS-combat but that's not entirely fair, as here it's just one half of the game.
Diplomacy on the other hand is more innovative. At first, it will seem it's just combat using different words. Substitute 'resolve' for 'health', 'composure' for 'block' and boom!, you have diplomacy.
But...there's more than meets the eye here. It's tough to put into words, but it has a different 'flow' to it. You have all these 'orbs' (arguments) circling around yourself and your enemy so it's obviously inspired by the Defect, while still very much its own thing. These 'arguments' you and your opponent put into play have their own 'health' and can be targeted, so the whole thing feels like a constant dance between states of mind. I wouldn't know how much of this is intentional, but the system somehow ends up feeling like having a heated argument with someone. Kudos for that!
The negative here is that, in the interest of theme (more on theme later), you are bombarded with unexpected keywords. Composure, resolve, incept, argument, bait...it all leads to very confusing initial play sessions, and your first few negotiations feel like a grind. But once I got past that initial hurdle...I personally love what they did with the system.
It's not perfect, mainly because you get so much concurrent effects that at times it's hard to understand what just happened. The UI is good, maybe as good at it could be, but a log is very much needed. And I think they could streamline diplomacy, just a little bit....maybe a few of the less impactful keywords can be taken out.

Deck-building as a story-telling device
What might not be immediately apparent once you start playing is how much effort Klei is putting into using deckbuilding as a story-telling device. The theme part is both good and...well, not bad but something that's worth taking into account, depending on your tastes.
Some simple examples:
- you can eat pancakes to heal (Fsscakes, technically), but that'll put a temporary card called 'Bloat' into your combat deck, which does nothing but can be expended for 2 'energy'. This of course literally bloats your deck.
- if you kill someone you could have spared, your Negotiation deck is subtly shifted for a while: cards tagged Diplomacy are a bit less effective and cards tagged Agressive are stronger. Y'see, you're now more intimidating.
- if you befriend someone after helping them in a quest you can give them a sum of money to make them love you, granting you a permanent effect (as long as you don't make an enemy of that person later on); obviously this is the same thing mechanically as buying a relic to complement your deck, but instead it's wrapped into the whole web-of-relationships system.

So it's nothing earth-shattering, but it's a nice effort and it leads to some 'aha' moments when you realise what they're trying to represent with some of these effects they throw at you.

The downside of course is that the story aspects can get in the way of the card play, especially after your N-th run (there's a lot of *randomised* content here and they keep adding more, but it's far from endless). Thankfully they seem aware of this, so there's a neat solution: you can dramatically speed up the 'narrative' portion of the game. There's also the recently introduced 'Brawler' mode for the first character, which is basically 'No-story' mode: a series of random encounters to test your deckbuilding skills against.

It's worth mentioning at this point that, in true StS-fashion, nothing is hidden from the player. So whenever you make a decision, you will understand the mechanical consequences (story-wise, there might be some unexpected twists in there).

* randomised in the sense of quests or storyline branches selected randomly from a hand-crafter set.

The nitty-gritty
All this is well and good, but if the card play is not well implemented the whole game crashes like a house of...well, cards. Thankfully, they did a good job here too. Is this as brilliantly balanced as StS? It's too early for me to tell, but my gut feeling is that it's not. And I don't think it ever will be, there's just too many things happening outside of your deck for that. But that's not a problem, it plays well, the explosive combo potentials are there for you to tease out, it seems fair and so on.
I want to highlight an interesting difference to how StS approaches deckbuilding though:
Each character has cards organised in sets based on their effects. For example, cards that let you discard other cards, cards that give bonuses when discarding and cards that are more powerful if discarded are grouped in a set (these sets are what you progressively unlock by finishing runs). The interesting thing is that when starting a run, you can select which sets you want active. I don't want to spoil too much of the character builds here so I'll an example from StS: I wanna try out a Silent build focused on 0-cost attacks, so I disable cards dealing with Poison.
Personally, I would recommend playing with all available sets active, as the feeling of satisfaction comes from taking an unlikely grouping of random cards and making a viable build out of it. But it's a neat option to have and it will probably make the game more accesible.
Another interesting idea is how card upgrading works. Each card need to be played X times before upgrading it. Once you satisfy that, you are presented with 2 possible upgrades, selected randomly from a pool of around 4-5 as far as I can tell. So there's some variety there, some of that feeling of 'making do with what you were given' and a neat sense of constant progress.

The card play will seem like simplified StS at first (especially combat), but that's because all the interesting cards are locked behind advancing. Once you unlock some of the sets, I think most StS veterans would be pleased with the potential builds to be explored here.

Overall
If you got to this point, you probably realise I'm quite taken with the game. The intriguing diplomacy system, neat UI, art direction and level of polish make it a worthy succesor of StS in my eyes.
Does it have that same potential of run after run after run, until it's morning already? Probably not, but there's a lot of game here either way.
So if you want something somewhat like StS but with way more 'game' around the card play and some truly fresh ideas, this is a must.
