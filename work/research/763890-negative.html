Posted: 16 July, 2021
For its first ten hours, Wildermyth is charming and great. But, like the Wizard of Oz, once you've played enough to "see behind the curtains" into the game's narrative and gameplay systems, the magic and charm swiftly atrophy. Wildermyth is, ultimately, wildly overrated.

I have a piece of advice I often tell my creative writing students: "If a detail is important enough to put in once, it's good enough to bring up again." If, when introducing a character, you spend several paragraphs describing the tattoos covering her body, then those tattoos should be a pivotal aspect of her character. They should be brought up again later by the character herself or others. You do this because it trains the reader to pay attention; it imbues even mundane observations with intrigue.

This is, in fact, a form of Chekhov's Gun, from the famous playwright Anton Chekhov who said: "If in the first act you have hung a pistol on the wall, then in the following act it should be fired. Otherwise don't put it there."

Wildermyth, however, repeatedly hangs pistols on walls, only to completely ignore them later. For example, one of my primary characters that I've used across all my campaigns is a warrior with a WOLF HEAD. You would think, perhaps, that this might constitute a rather conspicuous detail. You might think other characters that she recruits would ask, "Uh why in the world do you have a wolf head?"

But you would be wrong. My character having a wolf head didn't change anything in the story whatsoever. Because Wildermyth's individual story hooks and vignettes are largely self-contained. So a given story arc might have multiple parts that span several chapters, but story arcs never (that I saw) interact with each other.

So actual character and world development feels random and disconnected, and as a result I found Wildermyth's story-telling to be uninteresting and emotionless. Sure your characters can "fall in love" but all that really means is some tag in the UI, with minor gameplay and story effects. A sad definition of love, if you ask me.

Which leaves us with the game itself, the mechanics. And what's there is decent... but there isn't much there.

I have this term - "indie threadbare effect" - that I find myself using all the time, to describe indie games which push replayability far beyond the game's mechanical depth and breadth. That also describes Wildermyth quite well.

There's 8 or so scripted campaigns, as well as the choice to pursue a procedural campaign. Your characters within these campaigns can become legacy heroes that can be re-used in other campaigns and even advanced to higher tiers. So it looks like there's a lot of replayability.

But the game's mechanical content lasts about 2 campaigns. There are THREE classes, each with 10-15 abilities (passive and active) as well as a small pool of general abilities. There's also 10-15 spells total for the mage class. By the time you've completed your second, maybe third, campaign, you'll have seen and done it all.

Of course every person's sense of novelty is different... but I was shocked at how quickly the gameplay grew stale. By the third campaign, it's fair to say that 99% of the battles were mechanically identical to ones I'd already fought before. And even the procedurally chosen mini-vignettes started frequently repeating.

The game has an undeniable charm but at this stage, I'd say it's more of a platform than anything. It desperately needs more content. More classes, more abilities, more vignettes. But while all of that will improve the GAME part of Wildermyth, I'm not sure anything will fix its fundamentally flawed story-telling. In a good story, details need to matter. If my main character's head gets replaced with a WOLF SNOUT, that should matter. It should inform every future interaction that character has.

But in Wildermyth details don't matter.

In short, Wildermyth is just a very light game. Story-lite, gameplay-lite. It's quite breezy. Which may be just what some people want. But anyone interested in depth in their gameplay and story will want to look elsewhere.

This game is praised by other for its randomly-generated storytelling, but the problem is you can’t tell a GOOD story with procedurally-generated sentences that are randomly strung together.

A good story involves continuity. If a theme or element is introduced, it should be reinforced and expanded upon later. For example, if a character is a talented musician, it’s a part of their backstory; it’s part of how they cope with adventuring; it affects how other people see them. And over the course of the story, there should be times where that unique trait can be used to solve problems or get them into trouble.

That’s DnD roleplaying 101. (I only bring this other because other people praise Wildermyth for being like DnD roleplaying.)

Instead, the game generates random elements and then forgets them. In the epilogue, one of my characters “helped So-and-so design cloaks reminiscent of Cape Hook.” This character at no point in the entire game was ever shown to have an interest in fashion, have any connection to random location “Cape Hook”, or have any connection to the person she was helping. It was just madlib where three random nouns were inserted into a pre-written sentence.

Unfortunately, that’s how all the story events play out. There are character parameters that guide the madlib selection—a “brash” character will pull madlibs from the “brash” bucket—but they still end up with a series of unrelated scenes. There’s no sense of continuity or climactic progression.

On the gameplay front, the tactics-style battles are simple but enjoyable. After 5 hours, they’re starting to feel repetitive, but people have different tastes in terms of complexity.

The overworld map gameplay, on the other hand, is paper-thin. There’s just…nothing there. Because of the way “work” towards scouting/building is calculated, the best strategy is simply to group your main squad together and march to the goal, scouting, building, and fighting as a group everything in your path.

I tried my best to like this game, playing through the tutorial and a five-chapter story campaign. Unfortunately I found the writing, which is the most important part, to be underwhelming.

People compare this game to XCOM but there is very little in common. This game is all about the story. Things like stats, gear, combat, tactics are an afterthought, seemingly by design. That is fine on its own. The game starts with detailed character creation where you customize not only stats/appearance, but also personality. The game features short segments for every encounter/plot where your characters interact and talk. Their personality influences what they say and do, while the player gets to steer the plot a bit. From a glance this sounds awesome.

The problem is that the various character interactions and events come out of the blue. Two characters suddenly have a moment while travelling, and now they're in a relationship (after never having talked before). Two other characters become rivals all of a sudden. Some event comes up and they talk like they've known each other for years, after having been in two fights together. The game expects you to "fill in the blanks" between the story vignettes. It's a game of head-canon. In games like XCOM or Darkest Dungeon where you witness the characters fight through many gruelling missions, you would happily establish the head-canon on your own, shipping your favorite characters etc. Here there is not enough "game" for that head-canon to develop, at least not for me.

Each chapter features 5-8 combats which occur over a timespan of several years (??). Then at the end of each chapter you skip another 10 years (?????). I did not find myself feeling much attachment or interest in the characters because I hardly got to see them do anything. It's like the game gave me tiny slivers of insight into what happened during all those years, and expected me to fill in the blanks. I'm sure many people are into that sort of thing, but for me it felt shallow. By the very end you start to have some interest in your characters, but then they retire because each chapter skips 10 years and they get old. The game even features your characters having children, who eventually join the party, but if I was not yet invested in their parents, then how does adding more characters to the mix help?

Another major flaw is that the dialogue is all sorts of weird. The characters all talk in a similar fashion, with different coats of paint depending on if they're grumpy or friendly or funny. Everyone speaks in a jarring, short, slightly snarky tone at all times. Start sentences abrupt. Like something you'd find in twitter posts or online dating profiles. They all sound the same after a little while. Then they have kids and the kids also sound the same. When the game switches from your characters to NPCs/plot events, it instead becomes very verbose and prosaic, to the point where it almost feels pretentious. I'm sure the writing would feel more impactful if there was more time spent in building up the characters or plot encounters. As it stands, things go by so quickly that I barely follow the story along because it does not seem interesting enough.

Overall, I think this game is good if you really just want a blank slate to fill in. For those looking for a game with more "meat on the bones", whether in gameplay or in story, I would not recommend it.

My first run through the first (tutorial) campaign was a blast. I enjoyed the cheeky writing and the sometimes heartfelt moments. It felt like I was reading some of the old web-comics I used to enjoy. I had moments of sorrow and loss, and moments of triumph. I felt things, and this was good. I also enjoyed the combat mechanics at first which seem to heavily favor mindful positioning and well... not much else.

Yes, after about ten hours everything seems to blur into one homogeneous, dull shade. Combat loops are essentially the same for every battle and every class (of which there are three). Classes do not change hardly at all from play-through to play-through because there are only a small handful of potential abilities to choose from on level up. So maybe three main archetypes per class (and that is being very generous).

Dialogue and banter remains recycled through every campaign. I expected to discover new story arcs on subsequent campaigns but I found maybe three I hadn't experienced in the first 8 hours later on. (Mainly pet and transformation arcs.) Every character is essentially the same personality. The Legacy system ultimately therefore adds nothing of import to the game. Every character can be boiled down to a handful of dice rolls.

So, no, I do not recommend this game at it's current asking price. I have spent 16 hours in game at the moment and I feel I have played it to absolute death. The heart is there but the guts just ain't.

Imagine XCOM, but with all your squad members having their own personalities, personal histories and relationships, so you can get even more emotionally attached to them. They don't just die on the battlefield, they also form friendships, romances and rivalries with each other, grow old, have children, retire and then have their kids take their place in the squad. Sounds good on paper, right? I believe it could be great in practice too. Unfortunately this game suffers from a bit too many poor design choices to prove that though.

1, The tactical battles are way too simplistic. The battle maps are very small, there's no high ground, cover rarely plays any role, and the enemies' only tactic seems to be to bumrush you. The only difference between a very hard battle and trivial one is whether all the enemies all spawn in one room, or several separate closed off spaces.

2, The game is extremely repetitive. There's 5 different enemy factions, each with a respectable list of units, which is good - except you'll mostly be fighting only one in any given campaign, and the battle roster always just seem to follow the "one of everything" rule, so you end up fighting same set of enemies every single battle. The same goes for the personal side quests and pre-battle events told through comic strips and dialog options, which are supposed to add that story-driven flavor - there just isn't enough of them. I only played through two campaigns and I've already seen a lot of them repeating. (Also, not sure if it's a translation issue or something else, but some of them are straight up nonsensical.)

3, There's no variety to the character builds either. There's only 3 classes, and while in theory you could optimize the same class for different playstyles through the skills you pick at level-ups, in practice you have to choose those from a randomized list of 4, so you just end up picking the same ones you know to work alone. You can't build synergies when you don't know what you'll be able to pick next. (And I haven't been able to get a character beyond level 5 before they retired or the campaing ended, so you don't get that many picks either.)

Overall, it's a great concept, kinda' like a lovechild of XCOM and Crusader Kings 2, two of my favorite games. But if you're making something like this you have to make sure to add a lot of depth and player choice, or you'll just end up with a gimmick disguised as a game.
