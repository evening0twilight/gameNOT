The game is a lot of fun, great narrative and atmosphere. However there are some criticisms i have in regards to the general design and play experience. I'll give a bullet list and then go in depth on some problems i had.

Pros:
- Great storytelling
- Very nice atmosphere
- Fun survival management

Cons:
- A lot of clicking through repeated dialog, could use a skip or fast-forward for parts you've already done (EDIT: patch 1.2.2.0.0 Fixes this as you can hold ctrl key to fast forward dialog)
- A branching decision tree type load system that doesn't work with the survival mechanics and isn't focused around clear 'decision points'
- Research points.. they don't do anything.
- A few UI bugs outstanding

So to talk over the cons listed. I found that my experience was that i played my first run, got game-overed a couple of hours in, and then had to start from the beginning because my issue wasn't any one particular decision made, but rather just not having known how to optimise resources from the beginning. This meant that i had to click through a huge amount of text that i'd already seen just to reassign some rations differently next time. From what I've seen this is a common issue, and leads into the other major criticism i have.

The game uses a branching system for loading your save, the implication being that these points are where you can make different decisions to effect your run. BUT, that's not really how this game works at all. Being a survival game the major thing you're fighting is gradual erosion of your supplies or loss of crew, and that isn't an easy load back to fix. This usually means that in case of a game over you have to load a long way back and not just to the last "decision point". This combined with the fact that there aren't really branching narratives so much as a couple of different but very similar outcomes towards the end mean that your decision tree is just one or two game overs and then a straight line to finish once you learn how to optimise resources. Not only that but because the game doesn't really have much in the way of a branching narrative there really isn't any motivation to come back and try something different at certain points either as you'd be clicking through so much repeated dialog until either hitting a game over or reaching the ending.

Finally the research points are inconsequential, I've asked around and it seems they give you a +1 to a specific dialog ONLY if you don't have enough loyalty, making them almost entirely meaningless in the game. This is rather silly given that they require allocating workers and sacrificing resources to obtain, and are never really clearly explained.

Ultimately i really like the game, but i think the branching load system was a bad design choice, and exists purely for the sake of metaphor (somewhat ironically). The core survival system and narrative are very fun, but include some weird additions like research points, and game overs unfortunately make it so you end up having to repeat huge segments of text until you're no longer having fun. All love to the devs and definitely not trying to be a negative nancy, but thought i'd express my honest thoughts.

The good thing about this narrative manager is that it's not terribly hard — games of the genre, like Help Will Come Tomorrow or Gods Will Be Watching tend to be, they often revel in the feeling of "you WILL fail and get an imperfect ending, that's the point of suffering." Not The Pale Beyond: I saved everyone on my first playthrough and was close to a perfect ending.

Not that it was trivial and mindless. The difficulty is balanced all right, if slightly on the lower side. I do feel that the game would benefit from being slightly harder.

On the other hand, the game has insufferable bugs. 30% of the time interfaces would glitch and either refuse to let me assign people and resources or not show it. You can usually go back and try again, but it gets really annoying.

Quality control also begins to falter by the end, with missing punctuation and pieces of dialogue missing (I think). The Pale Beyond is just, overall, shoddy and rough around the edges. There's no getting away from this.

Some things happen or not happen depending on your earlier choices without you knowing.

But the writing is good, and that's more important. You follow the story of a small crew that gets stuck in ice and needs to survive until rescue amidst falling temperature and morale, hunting penguins and seals for blubber and meat. The crew is just big enough to allow for some sacrifice, but small enough for you to remember every sailor — so they're not mindless pawns you throw around missions.

As for the main characters, leaders whose loyalty you need to win and whose stories you follow, they are a mixed bag. I liked some — like the doctor whose changes you get to see (and influence!), or a distrustful illiterate carpenter-poet. Others have left me scratching my head. A cook who only every talks about food? Really? It's like a fat kid from an 80s movie whose only personality is being fat.

Still, even if some of the characters are misses, none are annoying, so slowly winning their trust and helping everyone survive is fun.

As for the ending... either I'm missing something, or it's kinda underwhelming. The idea with magic peaches that let you change the timeline — i.e. rerun the game for better results is less fun and ludoharmonious that the developer team thinks. I don't feel like this game needed this particular fourth wall hump.

But overall, I recommend The Pale Beyond. It's just the right size, it doesn't pull big "gotchas" on you, it's beautiful and has a great theme. What more is there to ask?

Do you remember the faces of the people that died because of you? He asked me. Every single one, I answered. He was right; it's not the choice that is difficult. It's living with the consequences.

I wasn't sure what to make of The Pale Beyond, at first. The closest game I'd compare it to is This War of Mine, but make no mistake - this is a very text-heavy affair. And the way your choices weave into the gameplay, the character dialogue, and the general direction of the story? Oh, let me tell you about that.

If you've played Disco Elysium, you probably remember how impossibly impeccable its script is when it comes to adapting to what you've said and done; how some small remarks can almost turn into arcs that develop and resolve in a satisfying manner. Well, here it's perhaps not as expansive, but it's still the same amazing feeling.

Listen, Robin: they will remember what you said. Whether you stay true. Who you chose to help, why, and how exactly. It never feels like you're just exploring a dialogue tree, and it's never obvious which dialogue option will do what. You need to intuit it. Feel what really makes your crew tick. Can you do that, Shaw?

But then there's the... issue. A really big one. It's almost enough to make me reconsider my recommendation. Almost.

See, the game's resource system pretty much expects you to sacrifice something, and then to regain it later. Which is fine - but it never mentions that. Your very first playthrough is pretty much bound to end up in a disaster because of mistakes you commited a long time ago. To avoid spoiling anything and being too specific: it's okay to sometimes not give enough. The crew is hardy, Shaw. As long as you care for them, they'll be fine; they might complain at the moment, but you'll find ways to appease them again. Calculated risks are an absolute must - especially earlier on, when things are... less deadly.

Now. I'm sure there's a question going through your head. Is... is it possible? Can you...?

And the answer is yes. You can. But some sacrifices must be made. Painful ones. Ones you might not be able to live with; but you'll have to. That, my friend, is the true meaning of leadership.

There's one more thing, Shaw. One of the endings provides you with a, shall we say, revelation. It's one of those that don't hit very hard at first. Only after a while, after you thought about it for a bit. It fully solidifies this game's central theme as "sacrifice". For what, you ask?

Well, what will you sacrifice for, Robin?

Listen. It's late. I need to retire for the night. I'm certain you've heard enough, and your mind is already made up anyway.

Bring them home, Robin Shaw.

I'm partial to survival games, but they often come off as cheap and unpolished.
But, so far, The Pale Beyond shows an impressive amount of consideration. Thoughtful, branching conversations, excellent music and art. It's very moody, and very well written. Easy to get lost in.

FINAL UPDATE, having now completed the game.

Gameplay balance is challenging and well thought out. You'll find yourself thinking about how to overcome the seemingly insurmountable odds even when not playing it. That said, it is remains balanced and this is a design and narrative accomplishment.

"What's the replacement rate on my fuel?"
"How many penguins is it gonna take to not starve to f'in death over winter?"
The tension is real.

There are some minor glitches and nuisances still left in the game, particularly in the latter half (ie med bay being unavailable due to narrative events, etc which can necessitate resetting a day) but it's all tolerable. Some bugs are about the only thing that is not immaculately polished.

Final issue, I do agree with other reviews that the ending, while amazing and satisfying, does arrive missing a bit of background. I would recommend to the Dev to do a bit more play testing to tie up loose ends here in a more narratively cohesive way, like they've done diligently throughout the rest of the game. (ie I never made those choices, so why is this under discussion? Did I miss something?)

That said, the pros vastly outweigh the cons here.

Overall, I'm very impressed. One of the best survival games I've played. When the Devs make another game I'll be first in line.

If a well written, well designed, atmospheric, survival game sounds like your cup of tea, don't miss The Pale Beyond. Cheers!

TL;DR: The Pale Beyond is a very good narrative survival game that manages to be greater than the sum of its parts. The mechanics are simple and lose much of their challenge once you understand them and the writing is not exceptionally amazing but the characters are genuinely well-developed and the overall journey is surprisingly engaging. 7/10.

I went into it almost completely blind, expecting some kind of survival management game and I got exactly that. But what kept me playing was the story, the characters, and the feeling that these people were counting on me to bring them home.

The gameplay revolves around managing resources and assigning crew members to different tasks. You’ll send people hunting, gather supplies, manage food and fuel, treat injuries, and make decisions that affect the crew’s morale and relationships. There’s also plenty of talking to people, and handling their personal requests. It’s a relatively simple system, but it works surprisingly well.

My first expedition was a disaster. I underestimated the importance of certain mechanics, made some questionable decisions, and eventually reached a point where there was simply no recovering.

And you know what? I immediately started another run. I genuinely wanted to try again. Not just because I knew I could manage my resources better, but because I’d grown attached to the crew and wanted to see their stories through. And this is where The Pale Beyond really shines. Every crew member has their own personality, background, relationships, and personal struggles. And as the expedition progresses, you get to know them, watch their relationships evolve, and see how they respond to increasingly difficult circumstances.

What impressed me is that nobody feels like disposable supporting cast. Everyone has something going on, and their stories develop naturally throughout the expedition. I had my favorites, but even the characters I wasn’t particularly attached to had interesting backgrounds and satisfying development.

The writing itself is just okay. Don’t expect particularly clever dialogue, memorable quotes, or outstanding prose. There’s no voice acting for almost the entire game, either, so you’ll be doing a lot of reading. But while the individual conversations aren’t anything special, the character arcs and the overall narrative are genuinely good.

The survival mechanics also contribute to that feeling. When supplies start running low, you worry about what might happen to the crew. And when you have to make a difficult decision, the consequences feel real.

The game does a good job of changing your circumstances throughout the expedition, too. Different locations, changing seasons, and increasingly difficult conditions keep things from becoming repetitive. Preparing for what’s coming can be just as important as dealing with what’s happening right now, and there’s something very satisfying about seeing an earlier decision pay off several weeks later.

Unfortunately, once you understand how everything works, much of the challenge disappears. On my second run I quickly realized that you can get away with using fewer supplies than the crew needs, and several conditions that initially seem disastrous are actually quite manageable. Once you understand the thresholds and know what to prioritize, the game becomes relatively predictable.

It’s still engaging, but eventually you’re playing more for the story than for the challenge.

There are some genuinely difficult decisions along the way, including a few where none of the available options feels particularly good. Your choices can affect relationships, loyalty, and how certain situations unfold. But if you’re expecting the constant moral pressure of something like Frostpunk, you won’t find it here.

The narrative is also fairly structured. Most events happen in roughly the same order between playthroughs, with variations depending on your decisions and which characters are still around. It also means replayability is somewhat limited.

Visually, the game is modest but charming. The hand-drawn character portraits are nice, the frozen environments look good, and everything is generally clear and easy to navigate. Nothing particularly impressive technically, but nothing that gets in the way, either.

As for the ending, I found it satisfying. Seeing how everyone’s stories concluded was a lovely payoff after spending so much time with the crew.
