Rolled into the first Gorgon campaign with no idea what I was doing. Wound up recruiting a warrior within the first chapter and I intended on making her a tank with a shield. Her name was Falula. At the end of the first chapter, as we fight the first of what would be many gorgons throughout the campaign, I messed up and she got maimed. Her shield hand was turned to stone. I lived with it, begrudgingly. I kept her in my party for the next several hours of the campaign. She couldn't wield a shield anymore, but she could still fight, and as it turns out, fight well. I made her a Paladin which had the passive of putting her into a "Guardian" stance after every one of her turns regardless of action. This meant that she'd attack the first enemy that walked by her. She held her own throughout several hours of play, pulled her own weight, and as I entered the last fight of the campaign against the Gorgon leader I rewarded her reliability and invested my resources into giving her the strongest one-handed mace I could craft. The final fight was long with many, many enemies. But finally as we're fighting the big boss man himself, as he walks into range to finish off one of my other party members Falula's Guardian procs and she strikes the ugly creature with a crit of the level 3 mace I had just given her. It was just enough to finish him off and so the day was saved. At the end of each campaign you can choose to carry over one character as a legacy character to other campaigns. I could have chosen any of the 5 other able-bodied characters I had. But I chose my stone-handed battle priestess Falula. This game rocks.

Wildermyth is just so good. I'm devoted to trying to get 100% achievements so I've been reading the wiki and planning my party compositions more, and normally that kind of kills the mystique of a game but I'm just stunned by the amount of content I'm still finding, as well as how well all the game's systems overlap to create something far greater than the sum of its parts.

Fundamentally the gameplay loop is extremely well designed, you start with a party of heroes who grow older through the campaign. Campaigns last between 30 and 65 years, and are split between a campaign map and individual missions, a lot like XCOM. Your heroes collect gear, experience, and learn new abilities, but they also grow older and become weaker as a result of their age. Unlike in XCOM or a more traditional CRPG where you tend to get a few good dudes that are indispensable to the party and carry you through a campaign Wildermyth has your heroes gradually become slower and less capable until grandpa finally decides to lay down the giant magic ice sword and retire.

Progressing through a campaign lets you try lots of different mechanics, recruit lots of different heroes, you can have highs and lows and the economic systems in the game are just complicated enough that you have good reason to use all the tools at your disposal without any one playstyle becoming overly dominant. It stays fun on multiple playthroughs because the combat is very well made and creates interesting situations, and the overworld is the same. The story told by the mechanics alone is good, but the game is also extremely well written.

The sense of lineage and personality that these heroes get throughout their lives is really incredible, there's more than a dozen personality types which each hero has varying amounts of, and the types of people your heroes are determine both what kinds of events and situations you get, as well as what outcomes are feasible in these events. On top of this each hero gets 3 "hooks" which are literal story hooks for a wide variety of personal quests or "opportunities' as the game calls them, these events are everything from simple whimsical asides that offer slight buffs, to characters making peace with their inner demon and becoming part shadow creature.

I'm on my 12th campaign at this point and I'm still finding new outcomes or even events I've never seen before. There's certainly repetition, but because there's multiple (sometimes extremely different) outcomes for most of these events it keeps it fresh, and sometimes it is nice when you get an event you know and you know the outcome you want as well, you can just hit F to skip all the way to the choice and if you don’t remember they’ve embedded links to the wiki in the game so you can read all the possible outcomes and pick the one you want. This is a REALLY nice feature if you’ve got a completionist mindset.

If you’re like me you probably don’t customize characters at the start of a game in something like XCOM, you wait until someone makes a name for themselves and maybe you give them a cheeky nickname or some cooler looking armour. I had a character I wasn’t very fond of, a ♥♥♥♥♥♥ wizard who I didn’t use much so he was permanently underleveled, and his name was Bolo. I renamed him Bozo, and then Bozo had a warrior kid who I named Bozo Jr. Jr has been extremely useful ever since and more than made up for their father. Jr is a high mobility warrior with good action economy who’s head was transformed into a crow’s head after they insulted a weird bird lady they met in a barn. I love that.


Wildermyth is good at making these narrative arcs, and since you can import characters from other campaigns as legacy characters into new campaigns they end up with this sort of mythic lore that follows them around, the last time I recruited Bozo Jr they got an introduction as a local badass who managed to 1v1 a gorgon and save their town, which is a special introduction that only legacy heroes get. It really is the little things that add up. I’d like to ramble about the characters I’ve made while playing, but that just brings me back to how good the writing is overall. Theres 5 enemy types; mechanoids, cultists, magic bugs, lizard men, and sea/forest monsters. Each of them get their own campaigns, all of which are pretty good. The first two campaigns are simple and light stories, decently told with mechanics that tie the narrative into the gameplay, but the third campaign takes it up a notch by gradually giving you procedurally generated characters who are related to the characters in the narrative, building up to what feels like a important conflict with history and consequences for the randomly generated party you’ve created. It is an excellent integration of narrative and gameplay, and the world it builds feels genuinely magical.

Another campaign sees you making choices for and occasionally playing as the primary antagonist, who is themselves a tragic figure that the player is brought into conflict with through realistic and dare I say gripping narrative. The lizard people want to build their own nation and that demands conquering human lands, the player has no choice but to oppose this as the other option would be subjugation. Its a surprisingly deep story about generational conflict. The characters who start that campaign will likely age out by the 5th act, by the end of that campaign everyone is fighting everyone else’s children for the same reason their parents fought. A bittersweet end to a tragic tale, in a game that doesn’t even have animation.

I really cannot recommend this game enough. Each campaign takes 4-8 hours to beat but can be played in bite sized increments, the combat is challenging and complex if you want it to be, it is one of the best gaming experiences I’ve had in terms of emergent narrative while also scratching that itch for a tricky turn based squad combat strategy game. Its like fantasy XCOM with more personality.

Early Access Review

This game was absolutely spellbinding for my first playthrough... but then it very quickly started to get old, after that. If you are reading through all of these reviews, looking to understand this game, you have probably noticed a theme, by now:

Originally posted by Literally Everyone:
All of the events started to repeat themselves, really quickly, and it diminished/ruined the fun, for me.

I don't want to just repeat this line that has been written a hundred times here, by now, but it basically sums up the biggest flaw with this game. So instead, I'll try to elaborate on the mechanics of WIldermyth a little so you can understand why everyone keeps saying this, and why it's such a problem.

As a turn-based RPG, this game is passable; not fantastic, but good enough that you won't get bored of it right away even though it probably couldn't carry the game entirely on its own. What makes Wildermyth unique and fun is the storytelling mechanic, which draws on elements from your playthrough to generate a unique story for your characters, each time you start a new campaign.

Characters are generated with a random assortment of personality traits, like cowardice, leadership, bookishness, goofiness, darkness, optomism, etc; I think there are about twenty five of them in all, and they are all given scores between 1 and 100. They generate relationships with one another based on how they met and how compatible their personalities are (friend, rival, lover, parent, sibling) which are rated from 1 to 5 based on how long they have known each other and how closely they have worked together in that time. They have histories that track what sort of experiences they have had, and they have "hooks" which define the sorts of things that appeal to them and can draw their interests. And of course, you have their age and how long they have been involved in the campaign. All of these things are used to generate the storyline.

Between each battle the game will check all of the above info for the party that is about to participate in the next fight (your heroes will split up and reform with different groups at different times in the game; they'll be around different people at different times in the story) and it will select an event from a long list of compatible events. For example: if a parent and their child finish a fight together, and the child is more serious than the parent, you may get an event in which the parent laments that their child does not call them "mom"/"dad" anymore. If you have two goofoffs in the same party, and one serious one, they may get into a conflict because the two keep f.ucking around and get the party in trouble, and that third guy won't stand for it anymore. If two rivals have high gambling traits, they might enter a wager concerning the next fight, which only one of them can win and which distracts from the quest. These events are told through comic strips that partly generate the dialogue based on the personality of the characters involved, and may prompt the player to make decisions or attribute checks.

The problem with this, is that you never really know, the first time, how much of this is fixed and how much is based on your characters. It is easy to suspend your disbelief and get invested in your characters, the first time, but once you start seeing them again on your second campaign, the paint starts to chip and you lose that magic. Then, you're just playing a somewhat below-average TBS game and you can't ever get that wonderment back from the first playthrough, again.

There isn't nearly enough content, here, for the gimmick to work more than once and it is clearly meant to be played over and over again. The legacy mechanics make that clear. BUT... the game is still in early access, lot's more content has been promised, and the devs are pretty regularly updating it on a roughly twice-montly basis, adding new events with every patch.

For that reason, it gets a thumbs up from me. My first playthrough, as I said, was spellbinding. I still remember my first party, and their stories, and even when I started to see the strings I still stuck around to play through the rest of the available campaigns (there are three, two more coming later). My second run was almost as good as my first, and it wasn't until the last campaign that I started to feel weary of it. That only happened because I had seen all of the events; the new stuff came too far apart for me to really feel invested in the characters anymore, and without the main gimmick the remainder of the game couldn't hold my attention well enough.

If the game looks fun to you, then I would wager that it almost certainly is. But you're only going to get about 5-10 hours out of it, in it's current state, before you lose that. If you really want to get 30-50 hours out of this game, then you should just wishlist it and wait till it comes out of early access. As for me, I'm going to put it away now, and come back to it in a year or two when I have forgotten all of the specific events, so I can play through the two upcoming campaigns from a fresh perspective, again.

It is hard for me to really articulate what makes this game special. The closest comparison I have to describe it is an AI driven D&D game. While there may be other games it can be compared to I dont have experience with them so I will have to defer to others to comment on the genre. With that preamble let me tell you why I think Wildermyth is incredibly special and deserves your attention and time.

The first thing I would say about this game is that you will get back what you put into it. When I first played it I thought the idea was cool, I had a lot of fun and I found some of the story plot lines original, funny, quirky and enjoyable. The second game I played (after the intro campaign) I found some of the same plot lines, hooks and jokes. Pair that with a more difficult campaign where some of my main characters died and it wasnt the experience I was looking forward to have. I was immediately disappointed and put the game away for about 4-5 months. I will tell you why I think that was a misjudgment now.

I picked up the game for a little variety about a month back. Now, since my last time I played there have been new campaigns added. I still havent gotten to those new campaigns. I just picked up my last game where I left off after I lost a main character.

It became obvious to me as I continued this particular story arch that death of your characters is a major part of this game. The intro story gives you a short campaign where my heroes aged at the end but I was able to complete everything without anyone dying. When this second campaign proved more difficult I was frustrated and angry when a character I put my emotional investment died. As I continued this story now months later I began to realize the scope of the typical campaign outside the intro story. These games occur over several decades. At the end of one season my whole starting party retired because they had become too old to adventure anymore. A natural and organic reality that is true to life. As I worked on bringing the next generation of adventurers into the story it finally clicked and I understood what the devs were making in this game. It is a multi-generational game focused on telling and retelling human stories. The mechanic of any hero dying or going into retirement is what puts them into your legacy and allows them to be drafted (from their young beginning) back into a different game where their personalities can be further fleshed out and they can live again in a new setting. Dying and retirement are going to happen, multiple times. That is the whole point. I started to view the game in this light for the first time and suddenly there was a depth and emotional connection I began to develop with every character that honestly I find difficult to put into words. You will just have to experience it yourself. There is something supremely satisfying in seeing a character of your creation learn, grow, form relationships then age and either retire or die defending their loved ones that is deeply invested in this game and it makes it unique on a level I have never experienced in a game before.. That campaign that I gave up on when I lost a main character? I continued the story. In it two of my original members fell in love, had two children who joined the group then both died in battle defending those children in a moment that honestly brought tears to my eyes as I watched it happen. But instead of being angry or frustrated I felt an incredible satisfaction. It was exactly how they would have wanted to go out.

The devs now have my undying devotion. I truly find Wildermyth a masterpiece of game design. It is both simplistic in its art and game mechanics while also as deep in its story telling capability. I have yet to use it but the ability to create your own mods, landscapes and story is incredibly exciting to me. I dont know if I will have time to use it but it makes for a source of endless adventure and gameplay when you include your player base in this fashion. I think it is a brilliant move.

Overall I cannot recommend this game more but I do so with just a little bit of hesitancy because I have found it to be incredibly special to me and I dont want anyone to dislike it. So please try it out, and please give it a good chance, unlike I did the first time around. If you do, you wont regret it.

Okay I bought this when it first came out and promptly refunded it. I always dislike when any game starts in a small window and it's never a good sign, I wasn't overly enamoured by the hand drawn graphics and the pop-up-paper style, and it seemed to me that the language wasn't quite right. But after seeing all the positive reviews and award nominations I considered that perhaps I had been too hasty and decided to give it a better shot. And boy I'm glad I did.

The narrative style is DELIBERATELY odd to give it a sense of aged lost mythologies and to create the feeling that the player is enacting his or her own fairy-tales that draw on many cross-cultural references. Moreover, the mixed personalities of your characters give them a surprising shelf life when you encounter them more than once due to the proc gen nature of a rogue-like and when each encounter is woven into a meta tale that is never the same it's going to take a hell of a lot of game time before they feel stale. This makes Wildermyth the only rogue-like that I've come across that continually produces a real narrative experience.

You are able to craft a variety of weapons and armour that increase the power of your characters along with artifacts and trinkets that can give you different skill sets altogether. But, more importantly, there are a variety of events that allow you to mutate your characters by body part. So you could gain a fiery arm that allows you to throw flames, a pair of wings that allow you to move farther and to go straight through other units as though you are flying over them, or an entire body focused on one mutation could eventually turn you completely into a werewolf. Neither are you restricted to a single mutation type. For example, I have one guy that has two arms of starlight and the tail of a scorpion...changes that were each made through narrative or "choice and consequence" following combat. A combat constantly made more interesting by the variety of these changes.

The mechanics are interesting, the fact that all changes to either your DNA or your clothing are reflected graphically is interesting, but it is the changing narrative which carries everything along that is the star of a pretty unique game with more charm and character than you can shake a stick at. Wildermyth has slow and steady pacing, isn't going to be enjoyed to its fullest by those that don't want to read, and is never going to satisfy anyone looking for a "rush". But if you like the idea of being part of a multitude of different Fairy-tales through the medium of a tactical adventure then there is simply no other game quite like this out there.

It is being nominated for many awards because it deserves to be.

*edit* After playing for more than 50 hours this is my GOTY by a comfortable margin. The writing is very close to poetic, some of the stories are so good that they stay with you, the music plays in your head long after you have stopped playing and there is plenty of room for a multitude of plays. Whilst I didn't, at first, appreciate the graphics I've come to realise they have their own charm and play a vital role in tying everything else together to make something that is more than the sum of its parts. In 40 years of gaming I don't think I've ever felt more like a game is a work of Art.

Wildermyth is a game I somewhat hesitantly recommend.

The game's primary selling point as a randomly generated roleplaying game with emergent storytelling is what will likely attract most people. To put it bluntly, this aspect is a mixed bag. You do grow attached your characters as they grow old and survive countless battles, but most of their characterization has to be inferred by the player. The actual dialogue is incredibly bland and fails to make characters feel distinct. Furthermore, the random events which compose most of the game's narrative are far too repetitive; the illusion of your story being unique quickly falls apart as you continue to play. There are also several aspects of the game's emergent storytelling that feel underdeveloped or completely missing. Character relationships are incredibly shallow, and legacy characters completely ignore their original campaigns in a way that can feel outright immersion breaking.

However, I still ultimately enjoyed the game's combat enough to finish all of its campaigns. The grid-based battles are simple enough to get you hooked right away, and the three hero classes actually present a surprising amount of build variety. Even if the actual writing fails to make your characters unique, the constant threat of permadeath can actually produce a great deal of emotional attachment to the units you've invested in. My warrior "MLG Waterbucket" was a true hero, and his sacrifices will stick with me for years to come.

Wildermyth is a silly, flawed game with a lot of repetition, but in a roundabout way it does succeed in creating player-driven stories. If somebody could use this title as a framework for a much more complex roleplaying system, then I think there could be something truly outstanding waiting to be made.
